00Overview

The brief was to take an existing light aircraft and repurpose it: we chose the Cessna 210 Centurion, stripped four of its six passenger seats, and re-sized the airframe to carry roughly 15–20% more payload in an enlarged cargo bay — for cross-country cargo, medical support or surveillance work — while keeping the original's proven low-wing, conventional-tail layout. Below is that redesign: the concept, the weight and wing-loading sizing, the tail design my sub-group owned, and — going deeper into just the vertical tail — the individual structural report I wrote to verify it against CS-23 loads.

01Mission & concept

A survey of nine comparable light utility aircraft (Cessna 208, 206, 172, 425, Pilatus PC-12, Piper PA-28R and others) showed that heavier, higher-payload aircraft cluster predictably on wing loading, cargo volume and runway performance — the Cessna 210 sits mid-pack on all three, which is what made it a sound base to redesign rather than a compromise.

The cabin's rear four seats were removed and the freed floor area — plus some of the original baggage bay — was folded into one enlarged cargo region, while the low-wing, aft-tail layout was kept for its aerodynamic simplicity and structural efficiency.

02Weight & wing loading

MTOW estimation

Removing four seats (≈22 lb each) freed cabin floor area; scaling that against the original baggage density (≈12.3 lb/ft³, from a 200 lb / 16.245 ft³ baseline) converted the enlarged cargo region into an equivalent weight, then the standard iterative sizing relation was run to convergence.

W₀ = W_payload / (1 − W_fuel/W₀ − W_empty/W₀)
Additional cargo capacity (new bay, ≈122 ft³)+784.7 lb
Converged design MTOW4,431 lb
Payload increase target15–20%

Wing loading

Stall, take-off, landing, climb, cruise and loiter constraints were each converted to an equivalent take-off wing loading; the most restrictive of the six set the design point.

(W/S)_TO = W₀/S
Design wing loading≈ 147 lb/ft²
Resulting wing area≈ 297 ft²

03Wing & tail design

Main wing

Kept the original NLF 0414F aerofoil — its gentle post-stall lift loss suits a utility role — and re-derived the 3D lift and drag with the larger span and area. Single-slotted flaps were added to hold take-off and landing distance close to the original despite the extra weight.

04Vertical tail structural analysis — individual report

Everything in this section is my own CW2 submission: building the V-n diagram, running a vortex-lattice aerodynamic case at the worst structural point on it, and sizing the fin's spar caps and skin against the resulting loads.

V-n diagram & design speeds

CS 23.337 sets the manoeuvring load factors directly from MTOW; stall speeds come from the aerofoil's C_Lmax, and the corner speeds follow from where the stall and gust lines cross the structural limits.

n+ = 2.1 + 24,000/(W₀ + 10,000)
Limit load factors, n+ / n−3.763 / −1.505
Stall speeds, Vs+ / Vs−72.0 / 100.7 KEAS
Manoeuvre speeds, V_A / V_G99.8 / 122.4 KEAS
Design dive speed, V_D202.3 KEAS

Six CS-23 clauses shaped which load case actually governs the fin's structure:

CS 23.351 — Yawing conditionsFull rudder deflection and rudder reversal in level flight, evaluated up to V_A.
CS 23.355 — Yawing manoeuvre loadsSteady sideslip with rudder held to maintain it, across the speed range.
CS 23.391 / 23.397 — Rudder & control loadsHinge moments at full deflection, reacted as point loads into the rear spar.
CS 23.441 — Lateral gust loads50 ft/s gust at V_C, 25 ft/s at V_D, applied as a transient side load.
CS 23.367 — Engine failureFull rudder at V_MC to counter the yawing moment from asymmetric thrust.
CS 23.303 — Safety factor1.5× on every limit load to get the ultimate design load.

Vortex-lattice aerodynamic loads

Modelled all three lifting surfaces in Tornado (136 panels) and ran the steady lateral manoeuvre at point A on the V-n diagram — the highest dynamic pressure at the structural limit — with a 10° sideslip to put a realistic side load on the fin.

Every case clears its margin comfortably — expected at this preliminary stage, where sizing is deliberately conservative ahead of a weight-optimisation pass. The Roskam-style weight equation put the finished vertical tail at 34.9 lb (15.8 kg), about 0.8% of MTOW.

05Propulsion & performance

Engine & propeller

The original 285 hp engine and propeller fell far short of the cruise thrust the heavier aircraft needed. Scaling power up and re-matching the propeller diameter for a 0.85 tip Mach number pointed to a shortlist of three replacement engines.

Both distances were built up from their standard four phases; the modest take-off increase and larger landing increase both trace directly back to the heavier MTOW and correspondingly higher approach and stall speeds.
